Capstone work in the Farm Out archive

Students in the IDT program may find a capstone project in this archive. There are several, and they are different kinds of work — producing text from a static scan archive, interpreting it into indices and a navigable repository, reading the whole run as a slice of community history, or talking to the people the columns touched. What follows describes the archive, its current state, and the four kinds of work in roughly the order they build on each other.

The work

Four kinds, roughly in order

Each one is a capstone on its own. Each also produces what the next one needs, so a student picking up number two inherits number one’s output rather than starting over. Nobody is expected to do all four.

  1. 01

    Produce the text from a static scan archive

    The archive is about 135 newspaper page scans and nothing else. Turning them into readable, citable text is a production problem, and an LLM does the transcription pass. The design work is the process around it: where a person checks, what gets recorded as uncertain rather than resolved, how a reader verifies a line against the scan it came from.

    Twelve columns are already done and carry instructive defects. Roughly a hundred and twenty are unread.

    Information Design + Technology
  2. 02

    Interpret it into indices and a repository

    Text alone is a list of pages. What makes an archive usable is the interpretive layer: subject and thematic indices, a chronology, registers of people and animals and places, search, cross-references. The output could be a browsable repository or a wiki.

    The interpretive decisions are the substance here, not the plumbing. What counts as a subject, how a passage gets tagged, and which of two readings of a paragraph you index it under are all editorial acts that a later reader inherits.

    Information Design + Technology
  3. 03

    Read the run as community ethnography

    Eleven years of weekly reporting on one village is a slice of time. Farm economics, wildlife, school funding, village events, and back-to-the-land households arriving in an old dairy community.

    The newspaper pages themselves are context. What ran beside the column, what was advertised, what the rest of the paper counted as news that week — the scans hold all of it, and the column is one voice on a page full of others. This work is entirely documentary. No human subjects, no review board.

    IDT, or a social science department
  4. 04

    Talk to the people the columns touched

    Subjects and readers are living and many are findable. Interviews, recollection, and correction — and correction matters, because the people who can check these transcriptions are alive and the archive has no way to accept what they know.

    This is the only track that involves human subjects, and it carries obligations the other three do not. Details below.

    Nursing or community behavioral health

The material

What the columns contain

From 1974 to 1985, “Farm Out” ran in The Freeman’s Journal in Cooperstown, written by Melissa Horvath and Doris Lange. It reported on a dairy farm in Cherry Valley. Each column runs in short passages: the herd, then a poem, then a reader’s letter, then a village notice. The column of August 31, 1983 moves from the economics of culling an unproductive cow, to skunks eating peanut butter off crackers on the porch, to a two-year-old finding a dead woodchuck on the lawn.

A cow is an economic unit. “Farm Out,” August 31, 1983

Other columns cover fifteen White Leghorns that roosted in an apple tree instead of the coop, a drake that behaved like a chicken, four years of Cherry Valley Art Day, homemade methane gas, and whether the school district should fund sports for girls. The recurring subjects are farm labor, local wildlife, village events, and household life.

❦

How it was written

Two writers, one page, a mark in the middle

The two women met weekly, on Tuesday afternoons from about two until four, and wrote in longhand on legal pads over beer or wine. In good weather they moved to the porch afterward and drank with whoever came by. The column was a standing appointment, and part of the social life of the place it describes.

They did not co-write. Every column divides in the middle at a row of asterisks, and each woman wrote one half. A reader who knew them could usually tell which half was whose. That divider is the most important structural feature of the run, and it is the one the current archive loses: the transcription renders it as a decorative mark, the same mark used at the top and bottom of the page, so a boundary between two authors reads as ornament.

Per-half attribution is an open question with a real method behind it. Byline order may track the order of the halves — the August 31, 1983 column is signed Doris Lange first and opens with the culling passage — but that is a hypothesis to test across the run, not a finding. Voice, subject matter, and living memory are the other available evidence.

Current state

How the twelve columns were made

There is no team of transcribers and no editor. An automated agent session produced them, working from two standing instructions in the repository: only the Horvath & Lange column, and always review the text. One pass runs from newspaper scan to committed HTML. The steps below are the agent’s, not a person’s.

  1. 01

    Search and enumerate

    Full-text search of NYS Historic Newspapers, scoped to the frjb Freeman’s Journal run, exported to a candidate list. “Farm out” is also an ordinary phrase, so the list holds false positives. The first row is dated 1941, thirty-three years before the column began.

    raw-data-files/
    farmout-results-1-99.csv
    n, doc_id, page, date, url
  2. 02

    Fetch the page images

    One full newspaper page per hit. The date-and-page filenames are the whole filing system. No manifest ties a scan to the search row it came from, or to the column built from it.

    farmout-raw-jpegs/
    “August 31, 1983, Page 6….jpg”
  3. 03

    Locate the column on the page

    The scans are whole pages, not clippings. Something has to decide which part of the page is “Farm Out” and where it ends. This is where the largest errors are likely, and the repository keeps no record of the decision.

    full-page JPEG in,
    column text out
  4. 04

    Transcribe

    Vision transcription gets most of a column right. Where it goes wrong cannot be determined from the repository, because inputs and outputs are not paired: nine of the twelve published columns have no surviving transcript to compare against a scan. “Always review the text” asks the agent to check its own work.

    columns/raw-markdown/
    farm_out_1983-08-31.md
    3 of 12 kept
  5. 05

    Headline, blurb, formatting

    Every published column has a headline and a one-sentence blurb, with passages set off by a dinkus and poems set as verse. Which of those came from the original page and which were supplied afterward is not recorded. The authorship divider is set in the same mark as the ornament, so it stops being legible as a divider.

    columns/*.html
  6. 06

    Write the page and commit

    Standalone HTML per column, a card added to the listing, pushed to main. Pages redeploys on commit. No build step and no database, so the archive stays readable as plain files.

    index.html
    GitHub Pages ← main

Nothing enters the repository with a record of where it came from. There is no front matter, no per-column metadata, no citation to the source scan, and no field for a word that is a guess. The listing page is prose, not a view generated from data. A metadata record per column would address most of this, and is where work in track two begins. One possible shape: 

# columns/1983-08-31.md
id: fo-1983-08-31
date: 1983-08-31
title: "Mazie Is Culled from the Herd…"
title_source: unknown        # original | supplied | unknown
byline: [Doris Lange, Melissa Horvath]   # order as printed
structure: two-part        # one author per half, split at the asterisks
source:
  publication: The Freeman's Journal
  page: 6
  doc_id: frjb19830831-01.1.6
  scan: farmout-raw-jpegs/August 31, 1983, Page 6….jpg
status: transcribed        # transcribed | reviewed | community-verified
uncertain:
  - "Maizie / Mazie — spelling varies within the original"
halves:
  - id: fo-1983-08-31-h1
    author: Doris Lange
    attribution: inferred     # stated | inferred | remembered | unknown
    basis: byline order       # untested across the run
    topic: culling a cow
  - id: fo-1983-08-31-h2
    author: Melissa Horvath
    attribution: inferred
    basis: byline order
    topic: wildlife at the house
    people: [Nora, Laura, Ben]
    animals: [woodchuck, skunks, ducks]

Known defects

Problems in the current archive

All of these are visible in the repository without opening a scan. Twelve columns exist because of the automation and would not exist otherwise. What the pipeline lacks is a verification step, and these are the symptoms.

The authorship divider is rendered as decoration.
The row of asterisks that splits a column between its two authors is set in the same mark used to ornament the top and bottom of the page. The most significant piece of structure in the run is invisible in the output, and no published column records which author wrote which half.
Two columns claim the same week.
The listing has a May 28 and a May 29, 1974 column, both about Art Day and the duck. The paper was a weekly and the results file records one issue that week. A third Art Day column is dated May 28, 1975. Nothing in the repository can tell which date is wrong.
The masthead disagrees with itself.
One transcript is headed The Farmer’s Journal. The published page says The Freeman’s Journal. A misreading survived into publication.
A reader cannot check the work.
No published column links to its scan, cites its page, or shows the original beside the text. Page images sit in new-pix/ unused.
The middle of the pipeline leaks.
Twelve columns published, three transcripts kept. Nine have no intermediate state to audit. A duplicate copy of one column sits in a second directory.
Whole years are unread.
1976–1979 and 1983–1985 are almost entirely untouched. The results file is named 1-99, so later pages of search results were never harvested.

Sourcing

What is checked and what is not

These notes were assembled by reading the repository and the live site. The claims in them fall into four kinds, and they do not carry equal weight.

Verified
Read directly from the repository or the live site: the file inventory, the twelve published columns, the three transcripts, the 99-row results file, the two standing instructions, the date collision, the Farmer’s / Freeman’s discrepancy, and the quoted line of August 31, 1983.
Arithmetic
Derived from filenames: roughly 135 page scans, a span of 1974 to 1985. Filenames can be wrong and nothing cross-checks them.
Remembered
The writing sessions, the legal pads, the split at the asterisks, and the fact that the halves were separately authored and usually distinguishable. This comes from Steve Schneider, who knew both women and started the archive. It is first-hand and forty years old.
Unresolved
Which woman wrote which half of any given column. Byline order is a hypothesis and has not been tested across the run. Also open: which headlines are original, what the 1941 row is, and whether any of the twelve transcriptions are accurate.

Track four

If you work with people

Tracks one through three are documentary. They can be done from the repository with no contact with any person and no human-subjects review. Track four is different.

One member of the household is ninety, frail, and living with dementia. That opens work beyond interviewing: structured reminiscence is a recognized practice, and it usually runs on borrowed material — era photographs, period music, generic farm scenes. Here there is a weekly written record of one household’s animals, neighbors and kitchen, dated to the week and already in short passages. Whether it helps anyone is unknown.

Contact runs through Steve Schneider, who started the archive and can introduce you to the family. He is a member of that family and not a neutral party in it. The human-subjects questions — consent, capacity, IRB — go to Professor Stam rather than to him. Four things to settle first.

  • Consent and capacity. The columns are published public record. Working with a person is not. That needs proxy consent, assent renegotiated at each visit, and a written plan for the days she does not want to.
  • Third parties. The columns name children who are now in middle age and neighbors whose families still live nearby. A 1983 paragraph about a two-year-old reads differently when that person is forty-five and searchable.
  • Framing. A diagnosis should not become the subject. The material is writing about a working farm.
  • Custody. If the village contributes memory, someone holds it in ten years. Decide who can correct it, who can remove it, and what happens after graduation, before collecting anything.

First task

Where to start

Read the twelve published columns. It is about an hour, and it is the basis for deciding whether any of this is of interest. A semester on an archive whose material bores you is a long semester. Nothing else matters until that question is settled.
