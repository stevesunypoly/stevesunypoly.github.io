Course Description

What This Course Is

STS 190 examines artificial intelligence as a system — built from specific materials, operating across physical infrastructures, embedded in historical decisions, and raising fundamental questions about mind, behavior, and human life. The course moves from where AI lives and what it is made of, through how contemporary models actually work, to the cognitive and social questions AI forces into the open.

The course draws on Science, Technology, and Society studies — an interdisciplinary field that uses the methods of sociology, anthropology, political science, economics, and psychology to investigate how scientific and technological systems are produced, deployed, and experienced. Social science lenses are applied throughout the course, and team projects are anchored in at least one of them.

AI is used to read, to think, to write, and eventually to investigate questions about AI itself. That practice is not incidental. It is the pedagogy. The course is organized around three questions that students return to all semester long.

FYS 111 runs concurrently, providing the credential scaffold: Learner's Permit, Agentic License, and Google professional certifications. Together, STS 190 and FYS 111 constitute a 5-credit integrated first-year experience in AI literacy.

The Course Questions

Three Questions, All Semester

Every module, every text, and every project returns to the same three questions. They are deceptively simple. Answering them well is the work of the semester.

  • 1What is the system?
  • 2What does it claim to know or do?
  • 3How do I know whether to trust it?
Course Goals

What This Course Is For

Broad, directional statements of purpose. The first three belong to STS 190; the fourth to FYS 111.

STS 190 · Goal 1 1
A historically and materially grounded understanding of artificial intelligence as a system built from specific materials, labor, decisions, and infrastructures — not as abstract capability or neutral technology.
STS 190 · Goal 2 2
The habit of examining AI through social science lenses — drawing on sociology, anthropology, political science, economics, and psychology — to ask whose interests AI serves, what it obscures, and what it costs.
STS 190 · Goal 3 3
The capacity to pose a disciplined question about AI, investigate it using AI as both object and method, and evaluate the results with appropriate skepticism and intellectual honesty.
FYS 111 · Goal 4 4
Foundational practices of ethical and responsible AI use in a college setting — understanding what AI systems are, how they work, and what it means to use them with integrity, transparency, and care.
Course Objectives

What the Course Is Designed to Do

Teaching intent. Instructor-facing. Module and session level. The first three belong to STS 190; the fourth to FYS 111.

STS 190 · Objective 1 1
Trace the development of artificial intelligence from the 1950s — Turing, the Dartmouth Conference, symbolic AI, the winters, neural networks, deep learning, the transformer — to the present, identifying the conceptual, technical, and social decisions that shaped contemporary AI systems.
STS 190 · Objective 2 2
Analyze AI as a material and infrastructural system: following a data request backward through its physical dependencies, and describing the spatial and temporal dimensions of the architecture of data — submarine cables, server farms, extraction, energy, water, labor.
STS 190 · Objective 3 3
Apply various social science lenses — sociology, anthropology, political science, economics, psychology — to examine specific claims, behaviors, and impacts of AI systems, with each lens explicitly named, its methods modeled, and its limits acknowledged.
FYS 111 · Objective 4 4
Scaffolding operational fluency with generative and agentic AI systems through the Learner's Permit and Agentic License credential sequences, Google professional certifications, and weekly reflective practice — developing the habits of documentation, verification, and responsible use that constitute ethical AI engagement in a college setting.

The final hour of every Friday from Week 6 through Week 12, and once more on Week 15. Each session is anchored by seven questions:
  • What changed, and when?
  • Who built it, and for whom?
  • What does it make possible?
  • What does it obscure or displace?
  • What data or infrastructure does it depend on?
  • What should we distrust about it?
  • How does it affect what we are working on?
Course Structure

Three Units, Three Projects

The course moves through three units of roughly five weeks each. Each unit produces a team project presented as slides with a live demonstration component, applying any social science lens to that unit's central question.

Unit I · Weeks 1–5
Foundation
What is the system?
Historical (1950s–2022) Elemental Spatial Temporal LLMs as Technology
Unit I grounds the course in the physical and historical world. AI is not weightless — it is built from rare earth minerals, runs on water and energy, travels through submarine cables, and emerged from specific decisions made by specific people at specific moments. The primary text is the museum exhibit catalog, read across its three chapters (Elemental, Spatial, Temporal). Week 5 introduces how large language models actually work: tokens, embeddings, attention, transformers, training, inference.
Team Project I: Using any social science lens, teams investigate one dimension of AI's material or historical infrastructure. What is the system, and who built it for whom?
Unit II · Weeks 6–10
Critical Framework
What does it claim to know or do?
Cognitive Behavioral Control & Regulation AI as Sociotechnical System
Unit II asks what kind of thing AI claims to be. Drawing on Agüera y Arcas, the course examines AI as a cognitive and behavioral phenomenon: what does it mean to call something intelligent, agentic, or mind-like? Governance and regulation cases from hiring, media, medicine, and policy anchor the discussion in concrete examples.
Team Project II: Using any social science lens, teams investigate a specific claim AI systems make — about intelligence, capability, or behavior — and evaluate it. What does the system claim? Is the claim credible? What is at stake?
Unit III · Weeks 11–15
Practice
How do I know whether to trust it?
Verification & Trust AI as Object and Method Living with AI: The Human Future
Unit III is the course's most hands-on. AI is used to investigate AI — posing a question, selecting a social science lens, conducting inquiry, and documenting the full process. The central question is not "what does AI do?" but "how do I decide what to trust?" Students build on their accumulated workbench archive and work toward the semester's culminating event.
Team Project III: Using any social science lens, teams pose a question about AI and use AI as the primary method of investigation. The project documents not just what was found but how claims were verified or trusted on stated grounds. Presented at the culminating event.
Primary Texts (proposed; under review)

Three Books, Three Layers

Each primary text corresponds to a different layer of AI — infrastructure, mechanism, and cognition — in that order.

Layer 1 · Infrastructure
Where is AI? What is it made of?
City in the Cloud — Data on the Ground: The Architecture of Data
Kokalevski, Lepik, and Hahl-Pfeifer, eds. · Architangle, 2025
ISBN 978-3-96680-038-9
Museum exhibit catalog. Three chapters: Elemental, Spatial, Temporal. The primary course text and anchor for Unit I.
Layer 2 · Mechanism
How do contemporary models work?
Hands-On Large Language Models
Jay Alammar and Maarten Grootendorst · O'Reilly Media, 2024
ISBN 978-1-098-15096-9
Selected chapters only — used as a visual and conceptual technical guide. Covers tokens, embeddings, attention, transformers, generation, and hallucination.
Layer 3 · Cognition
What kind of intelligence is this?
What Is Intelligence? Lessons from AI About Evolution, Computing, and Minds
Blaise Agüera y Arcas · MIT Press, 2025
ISBN 978-0-262-04995-5
Anchor for Unit II's cognitive and philosophical inquiry. Read as a provocation: a strong argument about distributed intelligence that students are expected to examine critically.

Infrastructure

The Workbench

Every student maintains a personal workbench — a structured cloud folder holding all AI-assisted work across the semester. The workbench is not a submission folder. It is research infrastructure, archive, and the primary evidence of practice. The full workbench is reviewed by AI weekly to surface patterns across the cohort.

Workbench Structure
ai-literacy/
  ├── transcripts/    ← every exported AI conversation
  ├── artifacts/      ← AI-assisted documents and outputs
  ├── synthesis/      ← synthesis documents; portable context
  ├── author/        ← human-only writing; no model open
  └── zip/            ← dated workbench snapshots

The /author folder is the course's unmediated space. Documents written here are produced without opening a model — pre-session reflections, first responses to readings, initial observations. It is the control condition: knowing what you bring before knowing what the collaboration changes.

Assessment

How the Work Is Evaluated

There are no tests. Assessment is through demonstrated practice, documented inquiry, and the evidence in transcripts and project archives.

Weekly Portfolio Submission
Submitted weekly against the rubric below. Includes evidence of RTW practice and verification decisions, and a brief update on the current team project. The full workbench is reviewed by AI weekly; patterns across the cohort inform class discussion.
Three Team Projects
One project per unit, submitted at the unit's close. Each project applies any social science lens to that unit's central question, uses AI as both object and investigatory method where appropriate, and is presented as slides with a live demonstration component. Teams of 3–4 students.
Attendance
Tuesday seminar attendance counts toward STS 190. Friday workbench attendance is split between courses. Attendance is the floor; participation is captured in portfolio submissions and team project contributions.
Credentials (FYS 111)
Learner's Permit, Agentic License, Google AI Essentials, Prompting Essentials, and one additional Google professional certification of the student's choice. All completed in Weeks 1–5.

Weekly Portfolio Rubric

DimensionWhat It Looks Like
RTW Practice Transcripts show genuine reading of model output, thinking through it — agreement, disagreement, redirection — and writing through prompting. Prompts develop in precision and depth over time.
Verification & Trust Claims from AI conversations are either verified against sources or explicitly trusted on stated grounds. Documentation shows which claims required checking and how.
Responsible AI Use Conscious choices documented about when and how to use AI, what to delegate, what to retain, and what the limits of AI contribution are in each context.
Archive Maintenance Workbench folders current. Transcripts exported and saved. /author entries written before AI sessions. Workbench reflects ongoing, not retroactive, documentation.
Team Project Progress Weekly update on the current team project: what was attempted, what the archive shows, what the team trusts and on what grounds, what remains open.
Agentic Development Evidence of AI operating as orchestrator increases across the semester. Early weeks: generative and analytical use. By Unit III: AI directing multi-step workflows toward a defined inquiry goal.
A Note on AI Use

Using AI in This Course

AI is used every week — to read, to think, to write, to investigate. The question is never whether to use it. The question is always: what did you notice about using it?

The professor uses AI to write, including materials for this course. This syllabus was developed with AI assistance. That is not a disclosure of compromise — it is a demonstration of the course's premise.

Work products, including transcripts, will be incorporated into the course knowledge base. Students may opt in to open-source their thinking and share it with the class knowledge base; the knowledge base serves as a collective, reflective view of the semester's inquiry. Learning is evaluated in part based on the evidence in transcripts. The transcript is the work. The artifact is evidence the work concluded.

Document everything substantive. Export every meaningful transcript. Write in your /author folder before opening a model. The documentation is not busywork — it is the record that thinking happened.
